The Long Fall bar, casino and job board

All Pale Blue Dot mockups (the tracker)

Ways to make money on a station that are not hauling cargo between markets: a shift at the ore sorter, contracts off the board, and the games in the bar. All of them are playable below with one wallet: the shift, four slot machines, blackjack, keno, Hold'em against the regulars, dice and the race book. Every game shows its odds and the house's edge on the table, because the player is owed the truth about a bet.

None of it is the game's own art.

The job board

Contracts are posted by NPC companies and stations (and, later, other players). Pay sits in escrow when the contract is posted, and is released on delivery: a contract can never pay out money that nobody had.

Blackjack by another name. Suits are ore, coolant, fuel and core.

Six decks in a shoe, reshuffled when the cut card (three quarters in) comes out. Dealer stands on every 17, soft or hard, and peeks for blackjack under an Ace or a ten, so you only lose your first stake to a dealer's natural. Blackjack pays 3:2. Double on any two cards, also after a split. Split pairs of the same value up to four hands; split Aces take one card each and cannot be split again. No insurance, no surrender.

Hold'em at the Long Fall

no-limit, blinds 2/4 · rake 5% of a pot that sees a flop, at most 12

Texas Hold'em against four regulars. Buy in for 200 cr of chips, stand up to cash the stack back into the wallet. The house's take is the rake, not an edge: over many hands you win or lose by how you play against them.

Pot 0
Waiting for a player
Buy in to take the empty seat.

How they think: each opponent estimates its chance of winning by dealing out the rest of the hand a few hundred times against the players still in, compares that with the price of calling (the pot odds), and then leans on its nature. Old Harrow waits for strong hands and rarely raises. Wren Askew plays many hands, raises often and bluffs about one bet in five. Officer Dace plays few hands but plays them hard. Bolt calls almost anything and almost never raises. Side pots are kept for all-in players; split pots share the odd chip from the button's left.

Hazard Dock (dice)

house edge 16.7% on every bet
Stake
The dockworkers' game. Two dice, three bets.

Under 7: 15 of 36 rolls. Seven: 6 of 36. Over 7: 15 of 36. Every bet returns 30 of every 36 credits staked, on average. It is the worst game in the bar and the most popular.

Odds and house edge

Every game here loses money on average, by design: gambling must never be a better job than a job. The variance is the point: a player who needs 400 cr for a repair tonight can risk 100 to get it. Limits are per station kind and per game day, so the casino cannot be farmed.

Where the money goes

Credits in, credits out

Proposed rule: the house's take is not destroyed outright. Most of it goes back into the world as wages (NPCs buying at the market, nudging prices) and as the station owner's budget, which posts bounties. A quarter leaves the economy as the cost of goods the station imports from off-system: that is the credit sink, and it scales with how much people gamble, not with a timer.

For the owner

Decisions / questions for the owner

Decided: the casino's games (owner, 2026-11-13)

The owner's words: "casino needs more slots themes, more complex slot machines, make them like actual rolls, or 5 columns with 3 rows with lines, the traditional 3x3 is also cool too, but gotta animate it good. I also want some black jack, keno (themed to our game of course) and maybe something more competitive like poker with AI".

So the bar has: four themed slot machines, two classic 3 x 3 (Reactor Roll, Smuggler's Hold) and two 5 x 3 video machines with paylines (Deep Vein, Aubade Frontier), all spinning as real reel strips; Orbit 21 as a full blackjack table; Star Chart, keno on the Aubade survey grid; and Hold'em against four NPC regulars with their own natures.

Taken: Hold'em takes a rake, with real credits

Options: (a) chips only, a practice table with nothing at stake; (b) a real buy-in from the wallet, the house taking a rake from each pot.

Recommendation taken (ask only with screenshots): (b). The buy-in is 200 cr, blinds 2/4, and the rake is 5% of any pot that sees a flop, at most 12 chips (three big blinds), as card rooms charge. It keeps poker in the same economy as the other games and makes the rake the credit sink, as question 3 below recommends for player tables. The NPCs' stacks are their own money; to stop a strong player farming them, a station's regulars should have a daily bankroll that runs out (proposed, not modelled here).

Taken: the returns the machines are tuned to

Recommendation taken (ask only with screenshots): classic machines return about 93 to 94%, video machines about 95 to 96% (they cost more a spin and swing harder), keno 87 to 90% (keno is a lottery, and it is the cheapest game to play: 10 cr a draw), blackjack 99.6% for a player who plays it right. Every figure is computed by the page from the game's actual strips, shoe and chart, never typed in, so a retuned strip cannot leave a stale number on a table.

Open: keno's top prize against the table limits

Ten of ten pays 50,000 times the stake (1 in 8.9 million draws), so a 50 cr ticket could win 2.5 million cr. Options: cap a draw's pay at the station's bank (the house can only pay what it holds), or lower the top pays, which lowers the return.

Recommendation: cap any single payout at the station's bank, and print the cap on the chart. The odds stay true; a station that cannot cover a jackpot says so before the ticket is bought.

Open: are the machines things in the world?

Recommendation: yes. Each machine is a block-built cabinet in the bar that the player walks up to and uses, with the reels drawn on its screen like the canvas here; the tables (Orbit 21, Hold'em) are furniture with NPC dealers and players sitting at them. Mockup next, if the owner wants it.

1. Is there gambling in the game at all?

Options: (a) yes, with real odds shown; (b) only for in-game items, never credits; (c) none.

Recommendation: (a), with the odds printed on every table as here. It is a credit sink and a story generator. If the game will ever be rated for young players, gate it behind a setting and replace the casino with the job board's games.

2. Which minigames ship first?

The ore sorter, four slot machines, Orbit 21, Star Chart, Hold'em, Hazard Dock and the race book are all playable here.

Recommendation: the ore sorter and Hazard Dock first (both tiny, both teach the economy), then one classic and one video slot (they share one reel engine), then Orbit 21 and Star Chart, then the race book, because it can later use real NPC ships racing on real tracks round the station. Hold'em last: its NPC opponents want the most testing.

3. Can players play each other?

Options: house games only; player-versus-player card tables with a 5% rake; anything goes at the smuggler den.

Recommendation: house games first; player tables later at the den only, with the rake as the sink (the same rake as the NPC Hold'em table). Player-versus-player gambling needs the same durable transaction path as trades.

4. How big are the limits?

Recommendation: per-game limits by station kind (trade hub 200 cr, mining outpost 50 cr, smuggler den 2,000 cr), and a daily loss cap the player can set, which the game honours.

5. Do mining shifts pay from the market?

Recommendation: yes. The ore sorter's pay per rock is the outpost's buy price for that ore, scaled to a shift, so a rich vein event or a crash in iron shows up in the job's pay too.
