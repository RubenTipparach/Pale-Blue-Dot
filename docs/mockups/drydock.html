Drydock and Hex Ship Builder

All Pale Blue Dot mockups (the tracker)

About this mockup: the owner's words, the idea, and which art is the game's
"we need drydocks to build ships too ... I would prefer ocean ships to be made of hex blocks as well so i, as a player can walk around as the ship sails about" (the owner, 2026-11-12)

An ocean ship here is made of the world's own cell: 2.833 m across and 1 m a layer, the cell you dig on every planet. Its rails, mast, helm and lanterns are the town's pieces, on cell edges and in cells as a house's are, so walking a ship is walking a building that moves. You lay the keel dry in a stone dock below the sea, build her layer by layer, flood the dock and float her out. Every number she sails with follows from her cells.

About the art. The game's own (byte-identical copies in drydock/game/, see its README): the ground is the terrain atlas's beach and fields sheets on 2.833 m hex columns with 1 m layers; the dock's floor, altars and copings are the settlement textures stone and granite, the keel blocks and masts timber, the decks plank, the ballast fieldstone, the gate and rails iron, the pump house stone and slate, all at their repeat_m; the Tern and the Loon at the jetty are the game's vehicle models (assets/models/vehicles); the hand and the post lantern in the hotbar are the game's icons. Mockup-only: the block icons and four textures (tarred hull strakes, sail canvas, the gantry's painted lattice, a deckhouse wall with a window) are pixel art drawn in code with the balatro-pixel-art kit (tools/art/drydock_art.py), sampled nearest with no mipmaps. The ships are built from the builder's own cells, so what you draw in section 2 is exactly what floats out of the dock.

1The drydock

What this shows, and how to use it

A basin of hex columns cut into the shore, seven layers below the sea, its walls stepping down in altars of granite. A caisson gate closes it to the sea, a pump house drains it and a gantry crane runs on rails along both copings. The ship sits on keel blocks. Drag the water through the dock's five states: she settles onto her blocks as it drains, lifts off them when it reaches her solved draft, and warps out through the gate past the Tern and the Loon at the jetty. Set the time to night: every floodlight, coping lamp, pump house window, welding flash and the ship's own lanterns light what is near them; the moon stays dim.

4Sail it

What this shows, and how to use it

Rebuilt 2026-11-13 on the owner's report. A sailing ship gets her drive only from the wind on her sails: the canvas she has set (↑ sets more, ↓ furls) times the force of the apparent wind on a sail at the angle the sheet holds it (← hauls in, → eases out), resolved into drive ahead and a push to leeward that her keel resists. She cannot sail inside about 40 degrees of the wind (in irons), is fastest on a beam reach, and with her sails furled she drifts to a stop. A rowed ship gets her drive only from her oars: W and S set the stroke (back water, shipped, slow, cruise, ramming speed), every bench pulls in strokes, the crew tires, and with the helm over the rowers pull harder on the outside of the turn and back water on the inside. An engine's thrust (W and S, the telegraph) adds to any sail. Pale arrows drifting over the water are the wind; the instrument at the top right gives the true and apparent wind, the no-go zone and the sail's state. On the water: a white dashed line from her bow is where she will be in a minute; the cyan dotted line is the GPS's route, with a post at each tack; the pale trail astern is her wake; amber buoys are waypoints. On foot, walk her deck (WASD) while she sails on, Space climbs a ladder or a hatch, G runs the gangway out when she is stopped.

A sea chart drawn compass-north up, as the game's map is, with the wind as arrows: trades from the east-north-east in the south, westerlies in the north, a slack band between. Click the chart to add a waypoint, drag one to move it, right-click to take it away. Then press Sail. You start on foot on her deck by the wheel, first person in her frame (WASD walk, drag to look). F at the wheel takes the helm, and the view becomes a chase camera behind her: A and D put the helm over (a rudder is a foil: she turns only with way on), ← and → haul in and ease the sheet, ↑ and ↓ set and furl sail (on a rowed ship W and S set the stroke; on a powered ship they step the telegraph through astern, stop, slow, half and full). F again lets go: she sails on holding rudder and sail while you walk her deck. Walk off the deck where there is no rail and you are in the water: with no one aboard her sails furl and she comes to rest; swim to her side and press F to climb aboard. Nothing steers for you: a sailing ship cannot point closer than about 40 degrees to the wind, so to reach a waypoint upwind she must tack. A waypoint counts as reached within 30 m, and the next one takes over. The GPS plans the route a sailor would sail, laid on the water as a thin cyan dotted line with a post at each tack (and on the chart, cyan dashes: straight when the mark can be laid on one board, close-hauled legs between the faint laylines when it is upwind, tack points as dots), marks the best heading on the compass bar (cyan) beside the waypoint (amber), and tells you which way to turn and how to trim. A curved arrow on the sail shows which way to swing it and how far, and turns into a tick when she is trimmed. It advises; it never steers.

3Walk the deck

What this shows, and how to use it

The same ship, at sea, sailing a slow circle. You stand on her deck in her own frame: the walker keeps its place on the planks while she turns, so the island and its lighthouse slide round the horizon. This is the cog's rule, already built (sail-the-cog 3.1 and 3.2: the walker stands on a craft's deck in the craft's frame, through a turn and under way). Drag to look; mouse look is raw. Pick where to stand from the places the builder's walk check reached. She sails because she is under way; section 4 is where you take the helm.

5How the numbers follow from the cells

A full cell is 6.95 m3, so it displaces 7.12 t of sea. The game's 1 g is 25 m/s2 (SURFACE_GRAVITY_MPS2_PER_G), and craft feel it. The builder integrates each cell exactly; the game floats each hex cell as seven sub-cells (its centre and six round it at half the circumradius, each about 1.07 m across and 0.5 m tall), which meets hull::float's rule of a buoyancy cell at most a third of the beam for any ship two rows or more in the beam.

6Blocks and pieces

Blocks fill cells; pieces stand in them or hang on their edges, as a town's do. The last twelve are the power levels: oars, steam, oil, fission and batteries. Made at the fabricator and the assembly bay from planks, timber, stone, canvas, rope and iron, and the engines from M20's chains.

7Six levels of ship power

One preset a level, each built from the blocks above. Each is the ship that pays for the next; the trader and the three-master are sailing ships for carrying.

8Saves, and what stays true

A ship is a stored record

Her stable ID, her cells keyed (q, r, layer) in her own frame on a flat axial grid (bow along +q), her pieces, her pose in a named frame, her hold and her flooding are saved when the keel is laid and every time they change. Leaving the helm changes who holds it, never whether she exists.

Every block is an edit

Placing or breaking a ship's block is an edit in the ship's frame through the durable path, naming its author, as digging is. The dock is world cells; the ship never is, so floating her out converts nothing.

The block set is versioned

A saved ship names her block set's version. A retuned block keeps its old numbers for ships already built, or migrates them forward on open; a removed block stays readable. A player's ship never changes shape on its own.
