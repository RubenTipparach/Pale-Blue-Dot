About this mockup

Roads (townsfolk design 10 and 11)

  • A road cell is an ordinary cell with a road top. Flat where the road is level; where the next cell of the road stands a layer higher, its top is a plane rising from the middle of the edge the road comes in by to the middle of the edge it climbs out of: one layer over 2.833 m straight through (19°), over 2.45 m round a 60° bend (22°). Never more than a layer a cell, so a road has no step. The slope is the walker's floor, as a street step's pitch line is: the Eye jumps readout stays at nought all the way up.
  • The hill road climbs ten layers, layer 1 to layer 11, one cell a layer, with one bend, beside a hillside that steps a metre a row.
  • Dirt track and gravel road. Packed earth, built with a shovel; gravel, from crushed stone, walked 15% faster (the chip says so while you are on it). Generated roads are dirt between villages and gravel between the large towns (recommendation taken).
  • The ford. Where a brook is shallow the road goes down into it a layer and up out of it again, on a gravel bed 0.3 m under the water, waded at full speed.
  • The timber bridge. Where the water is deep, a plank deck at the bank's height on posts down to the bed, with rails on both sides; a piece the world builds with the road.
  • Lit at night by lanterns on posts every sixth cell and at both ends of the ford and the bridge; slide the time of day past dusk.
  • In the game the roads between towns are planned once from the sites and the seed, each settlement to its two nearest within 2 km and the largest towns to each other, stored as records the world authored, and laid only on cells with no edit of yours: a road goes round your work.
  • Roads two and three cells wide are in their own scene: pick Wide roads: two and three cells under Settlement.

Wide roads (townsfolk design 10b)

  • Three roads side by side, one, two and three cells wide (about 2.5, 4.9 and 7.4 m), dirt to the west and gravel to the east, a cart and a townsperson on each. Seen from the knoll: One, two, three wide.
  • Why a wide climb cannot copy the one-wide rule lane by lane. The next lane is the next row, shifted half a cell along the road, so at the same place it is half a layer higher. Lane by lane, each cell rising from its own entry edge, the lanes would meet with steps of up to half a metre.
  • The rule: one shared plane for the whole width. Every cell of a wide road on a slope takes its top from one plane along the road, rising a layer per 2.833 m (19°, the one-wide road's grade), level past the foot and the top. Its column is cut or filled to the whole layer under the plane's lowest point on it, and a bed of packed earth fills to the plane. The surface is the same on both sides of every edge, so there is no step between lanes.
  • What it costs. Every other lane is half a layer off its cells. A lane in step with the plane is exactly the one-wide road cell (no cut, no fill). A lane half a cell along has its column cut a layer and a bed 0.5 to 1.5 m deep: against its natural ground the road is cut half a metre at the downhill end of each cell and filled half a metre at the uphill end. A two-wide road has one such lane, a three-wide road two (its outer lanes). Stand on a cell to read its cut and bed in the chips.
  • Cut and fill sides. The hill is a side-hill: higher to the north of each road (a cut bank of earth faces down to the road) and lower to the south (the road's bed shows as an embankment). See Cut and fill.
  • The walker crosses every lane diagonally up and down both wide climbs, and round both bends, with the Eye jumps readout at nought.
  • Bends only on the level (recommendation taken, ask only with screenshots). In a 60° bend the inner lane turns in one cell and the outer lane in two or three, so a bend on a slope would need each lane at a different grade, the inner lane up to three times as steep. Wide roads climb straight and bend on the plateau: the two-wide turns north-east, the three-wide south-east, inner and outer lanes named on the cells; a lookout mound by each bend shows it from above. The one-wide road may still bend while it climbs.
  • Junctions are level. A one-wide track leaves the three-wide road in a T, crosses the two-wide road below and meets the south three-wide road; on the plateau the one-wide road ends in a T on the two-wide's bend.
  • Wide timber bridges, two and three wide: a plank deck over the full width at the banks' height, rails only on the outer edges.
  • Lamps on posts every sixth cell, one side of a one- or two-wide road, both sides (staggered) of a three-wide road, and at the bridges and the junction.
  • Which roads are wide (recommendation taken, ask only with screenshots): gravel roads between the largest walled towns are two wide, three wide inside a large town's gate; village tracks stay one wide. A player lays any width by laying cells side by side.

Paving (townsfolk design 39 to 41, version 4)

  • The owner, 2026-11-22: "building roads shjould be like a way point system, where you have to mark road for pavving and then you walk up to blocks with gravel and pave it. to make gravel you need a grinder and mixer". Pick Paving under Settlement (towns.html#paving). Written up, not built.
  • Marking. Survey stakes are driven as waypoints. The cells between two stakes are found by the world's own road search, at most a layer apart, and marked. A marked cell is still its natural ground: east of the first stake the marks cross a one-layer step, which stays a step until it is paved.
  • How a mark looks. A band of lime dust about 0.9 m wide along the road's line, from the edge it comes in by, through the middle, to the edge it leaves by, its edges ragged and broken, grass showing through. It is painted on the cell's own top, so it is lit, shadowed and coloured as the ground is; in the game the terrain shader blends it in from a mark bit, no geometry laid on the grass. No outline.
  • Stakes stand at the waypoints: where paving stopped, at the bend, and at the plan's end. Each is one tapered peg of split timber, its point sunk, its head spread and frayed by the mallet, a strip of faded madder cloth tied under the head.
  • The preview. Past the last stake, the cells the next stake would mark show at half strength, aimed at the village track (the world's dirt track to the north-east), which is how a plan joins a road.
  • Paving. With the shovel in hand and gravel in the pack, F on a marked cell paves it, one gravel a cell; held, it paves the next one in reach on the shovel's beat. The paved run from the worksite is gravel road cells cut by the world road's own rule from the plan's order: the step at the first rise is a sloped cell, no step. Standing on a marked cell, a chip says so.
  • The grinder crushes stone: a board hopper over two stone rollers in a hewn frame, an iron crank, a plank chute to a heap of crushed stone. 2 stone make 3 crushed stone.
  • The mixer mixes crushed stone with sand into gravel: a bellied drum of staves bound with iron on two trestles, a crank, a trough of gravel under its mouth. 3 crushed stone and a block of sand make 4 gravel. Stone, sand and gravel lie in heaps round the worksite.
  • Dirt tracks stay the world's, between villages. The player lays gravel only; paving over a village track makes those cells the player's gravel.
  • Lit at night by the worksite's lantern, lanterns along the paved run, one by the bend stake and one on the track: slide the time past dusk. The marks are seen only where light falls on them, like the ground.
  • The town tree that walks a player through this is M33's Town tab (town-missions-tree).

Furniture (furniture, version 6)

  • The owner, 2026-11-23: "Add furniture recipes to carpenter as a seperate branch and make, add furniture to the town ... Make sure NPCs and player can sit in chairs, beds. Allow storage in drawers, closest, various chests. The player should be able to find things in chests", and "we need a carpentry station for furniture". Pick Furniture under Settlement (towns.html#furniture).
  • The game's own models. Every piece is drawn from pbd_core::furniture::model, exported to this page by tools/mockup_furniture.py: one shaped form each, every edge bevelled, legs tapering into the top they hold, a seat with a rolled front, a mattress and pillow that bulge, a chest lid that crowns with its bands wrapped round it. In the town's own textures (timber, plank, boards, linen, wool, iron), lit by the sun, the sky and the room's lamps like the walls round them. No outline.
  • Ten pieces, each made at the carpenter's bench and nowhere else: chair, stool, bench (two places), table, bed, chest of drawers (12 slots), closet (20), chest (15), iron-banded chest (24), crate (8). The showroom south of the house has them side by side; each has its own place.
  • Where a piece stands. In a cell, its back to one of the cell's sides, half a wall and a hand in from the edge, or in the cell's middle (the table). Several pieces share a cell when their footprints do not cross. Nothing stands in the 1.1 m by 0.9 m inside a door, on a stair, into a wall or under too low a floor: the same footprint rules lay a town house's furniture and check a player's.
  • Sitting and lying. F on a chair, stool or bench sits you, the eye 1.17 m over the floor; F on a bed lies you down, the eye at the pillow. A movement key, a jump or F gets you up. See Sitting at the table and Lying in bed. There is no sleep rule in the game, so a bed does not pass the night.
  • Townsfolk lie in their town's beds by night and sit at its seats by day; one body to a place, the player's included. See A townsperson asleep.
  • Storage. F on drawers, a closet or a chest opens its panel under the pack: its wells over yours. A click moves a stack across, a right-click half of it; its coins go to the purse. Try it in A chest, opened. A town chest holds goods that fit its household's trade; take what you like, nobody minds for now (stealing is for later), and what is taken stays taken: a town chest does not refill.
  • Lit at night by the hearth, two candles on the table, a sconce by the bed and one over the drawers, the lantern at the door, and the showroom's lanterns. Slide the time past dusk.
  • The Carpentry branch of the town tree that walks a player through this is on M33's Town tab.

    • Road planner (townsfolk design 42, version 5)

    • The owner, 2026-11-23: "Do you have a good mock.up for road building too? Should work similar to the house architecture but we can just use the regular fps controller for that since it's just moving from one spot to another to place down waypoints.gor road". Pick Road planner under Settlement (towns.html#roadplanner). Written up, not built.
    • Walking it out. With survey stakes in hand (1), walk and click (or F) to drive a stake where you aim, within 6 m. While the plan is open, the ghost of the finished road runs live from the last stake to the cell you aim at: the line of cells between them, cut by the road rule. A cell a layer below the next is a sloped cell, never a step; deep water is a plank bridge at the bank's height (up to 6 cells); shallow water is a ford on a gravel bed. Click the last stake, or Enter, to end the plan; click it again to go on.
    • Refusals. Only the cells that clash are red, the first named under the crosshair and in the panel: Too steep (the higher of two cells more than a layer apart, or two rises closer than the grade), Too wide to bridge, Open water (the mere), In a town (Wenmoor), A tree in the way (that one cell; fell it with the axe, 3), Too far (past 24 cells), Wide roads bend on the level, Too steep across. A refused stake is not driven and takes nothing.
    • Width and surface. R (or the panel's menu button) opens a small radial menu: width 1, 2 or 3, gravel or dirt, lamps. A wide road's lanes sit beside the centre line (to the left for 2, both sides for 3), every lane on one surface along it, so no lane steps against another. Dirt costs nothing and is slower; fords are gravel either way.
    • Bill and turns. The panel counts what is still to build against the pack: stakes, gravel (a cell), planks and a log (a bridge cell), and a log and a street lantern (a lamp). The aim's segment adds its share in blue before the stake is driven; short goods are red. Under it, the cells, the metres, the turns and how many are left.
    • Editing. Click a stake to pick it up: its two segments follow the aim, refusals and all, and a click sets it down. Click a marked cell between stakes to add a stake there and pick it up. X or right-click on a stake takes it back, if the two either side can be joined. Esc puts a held stake back.
    • Edits mid-build follow the house planner's rule: paved cells the new route drops are marked for removal under a pale chalk wash, and the shovel takes each up for the share of its gravel set on the slider; with Given back at once they go back to their ground on the spot. Edit mid-build moves the bridge leg's stake south off a paved run to show it.
    • Building it. With the shovel (2), click a marked cell in reach to pave it (a bridge cell takes planks and a log); Keep paving walks down the line paving one cell a stroke. The ghost goes as each cell is paved, so what is left is what is still ghost. A lamp is built with the cell beside it, every sixth cell on the left verge, and lights the road at night.
    • The record. Record shows the plan as the game saves it: road-plan schema 2, its waypoints, width, surface, lamps, paved cells and cells to take up. The cells are the hex line between the waypoints, so the plan reads back cell for cell.
    • In the overview (V) the same clicks work at the mouse, from any distance: the game has no overview, so it is the mockup's shortcut, not a feature.
    • Sliders. Ghost strength, the steepest grade (a layer a cell, every 2 or every 3 cells), the cost scale, the lamp spacing, the share of gravel returned, and what happens to dropped paved cells.
    • How it relates to the paving scene (design 39 to 41). Survey stakes stay the item, a stake stays a waypoint, the lime band and the stakes look the same, paving is the same shovel and gravel. New: the ghost of the whole road, red refusals, the menu, the bill and turns, grabbing and inserting, the mid-build rule, lamps, and the plan saved whole. A segment is now the straight line between its stakes, not a search, so the road goes where it was walked.
    • Lit at night by the worksite's lantern, Wenmoor's lamps and windows, and the plan's lamps once built. The ghost is a preview and is not lit, as the house planner's.

    What is modelled

    • The engine's cell. Every hex is 2.833 m flat to flat and every layer 1.000 m, the gold standard in CLAUDE.md. Walls, floors, stairs and doors are cut to it; nothing is scaled to fit.
    • Walls sit on edges, floors in cells. A wall is a 0.3 m (timber) or 0.5 m (stone) slab on a cell's shared edge, so a one-cell room is 2.5 m across inside, not a solid block. The town wall and keep are whole masonry cells.
    • A storey is three layers, 3 m floor to floor, 2.8 m clear under the boards.
    • Eleven kits change what a building is made of, not how it is cut: straw and mud huts, timber boards, half-timber, red, buff and clinker brick, fieldstone, ashlar, clay with a flat roof, and marble with columns. A hut is the one exception to the storey: one storey of two layers, with 1.9 m doors.
    • Ten settlements, from each biome's catalogue materials: a walled town and a village in the fields (limestone, oak), a desert town round an oasis (rust sandstone, mud brick, salt plaster, cactus), a tundra camp of igloos with a granite longhouse and a castle of ice (snow, cold granite, dwarf willow), a jungle village on platforms in three kapok giants joined by rope bridges (kapok, red loam, basalt), a swamp village of alder stilt houses on boardwalks (peat, moss, alder, reeds), a fishing harbour where the beach meets the fields (ivory sand, shell beds, driftwood, beach grass, and whitewash from lime burnt out of the shells), mound houses dug into a hill under turf behind limestone fronts with round doors (fields: meadow, limestone, oak), a cliff village on five terraces with rooms cut into the rock and a beacon on the summit (mountains: blue slate, granite, scree, lichen, sparse snow), and a town inside a mountain, entered by a tunnel, lit only by its own fires day and night, with a shaft of daylight over its pool; the overview cuts its roof away. Water is waded at half speed, as walking.rs has it.
    • Boats are solid, and the cog can be boarded. Moored boats bob on the swell but collide as a fixed hull to the gunwale, so you wade round them or step down into one from a pier. The cog's deck, castles and stair are floors, its rail blocks you except at the gangway, and the gangplank from the pier meets the deck with no step. Piers are walkways a metre over the water at the quay's height.
    • Lit at night. Slide the time of day past dusk. Every hearth, lamp, torch and brazier lights what is near it wherever you are, baked into the faces the way the engine's block light will be, and kept to its own room: a hearth does not shine through its wall. Every house door has a lantern, and house fronts carry street lanterns, closest together in the walled town. About half the windows are lit, with a candle in the room behind. Each settlement has its own lights too: braziers in the desert, torches in the jungle and at the ice castle, small lanterns along the rope bridges, the light on the harbour's mole.
    • Roofs never cut into each other. Every roof's plan, eaves included, is checked against every other when the town is laid out, and the keep and towers count as roofs.
    • Three stairs, all landing on layer lines. A straight flight crosses two cells and climbs a storey in 16 risers of 0.1875 m on 0.354 m treads (28°). A newel stair turns once per storey in one cell: 15 winders of 0.2 m, and 120° per metre, so a door at any layer is always in the middle of an edge. Street steps climb one terrace in one cell: 6 risers of 0.167 m.
    • The walker is walking.rs: a 0.3 m radius body 1.8 m tall, eye at 1.6 m, 8 m/s (14 sprinting), a 12 m/s jump under 25 m/s², a 1.05 m step for terraces, a floor from the highest of several footprint points and a ceiling from the lowest.
    • Proposed: a stair's walking surface is its pitch line, a grounded walker is held to a floor up to 0.35 m below, and thin geometry (walls, jambs, furniture, posts, people) pushes the body out along its face so it slides instead of stopping.
    • A newel stair ends in a 30° landing and a rail, so a walker who keeps turning meets the rail, not the well. A wider landing would take headroom from the turn below.
    • Light. Sky light is dimmed on faces under a roof, standing in for the baked skylight of the voxel-light change. Hearths and the forge are the only lights indoors.
